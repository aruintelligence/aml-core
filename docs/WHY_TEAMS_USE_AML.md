# Why teams would use ĀML

ĀML is most useful where interfaces are generated dynamically, policy matters, and a team needs more than screenshots or text diffs to explain what changed.

## AI product teams

Use ĀML as an **AI Interface Firewall** between model-generated intent and user-facing output. The model proposes; policy evaluates; the system records what actually rendered and why.

## Regulated or high-accountability products

Use execution receipts, signed policy packs, policy diffs, provenance, and runtime audit streams to make interface decisions easier to inspect and reproduce. AML is not a substitute for legal compliance, but it can provide stronger technical evidence about the interface-decision path.

## Accessibility teams

Use explicit reduced-motion, contrast-safety, keyboard, text-alternative, and cognitive-load declarations as an additional testable layer. AML does not replace WCAG or assistive-technology testing.

## Privacy and consent teams

Make declared personal-data collection and consent requirements first-class policy inputs rather than scattered application conventions.

## Design systems

Attach purpose, attention, privacy, accessibility, and policy metadata to reusable components so semantic changes can be reviewed alongside visual changes.

## Security / governance teams

Use signed policy packs, tamper-evident receipts, audit chains, Merkle inclusion proofs, and threat-model tests to detect unauthorized changes to the accountability layer.

## Developer-platform teams

Use the Meaning Gate in CI so a pull request can fail because the meaning or policy outcome changed—not only because syntax broke.

## The adoption thesis

Teams do not need to replace HTML, React, Next.js, or their existing component library. AML is designed to sit **in front of** or **around** the existing rendering stack.

The shortest version:

> **AI proposes. ĀML evaluates. The existing UI renders. The receipt explains.**
