# ĀML™ Intellectual-Property Protection Roadmap

This roadmap separates what the public repository can document from legal rights that require registrations, signed agreements, or confidential controls.

It is an operational checklist, not legal advice.

## 1. Preserve public provenance

Maintain dated public records for:

- major ĀML™ terminology and architecture releases;
- official marks and visual identity;
- protocol and RFC publication dates;
- release tags and commit history;
- major implementation milestones;
- field-experiment evidence with conservative claims.

Public timestamps can help establish publication chronology and provenance, but they are not substitutes for registrations or signed ownership instruments.

## 2. Keep the open-source grant clear

For software released under `LICENSE`, preserve the MIT grant and required notices.

Do not imply that a later business-model change revokes rights already granted for copies validly distributed under MIT.

Before placing a future commercial component in this public repository, decide and document its license first. Do not rely on labels such as “enterprise,” “official,” or “commercial” to create proprietary rights by themselves.

## 3. Protect the official identity layer

Maintain clear separation between open technology and controlled source-identifying rights:

- ĀML™ and related claimed marks;
- official logos and badge artwork;
- official compatibility and certification-style identity;
- “Powered by ĀML™” and OEM/co-branding programs;
- official authorization credentials;
- official trust roots and authorization registries.

Use `TRADEMARKS.md`, `OFFICIAL_MARKS.json`, and written commercial agreements as the public/control layer. Do not use ® unless registration for the relevant mark and goods/services has actually issued.

## 4. Evaluate trademark registration

With qualified trademark counsel, evaluate applications for the marks most central to commercial identity and likely revenue, including the core ĀML name and selected official compatibility/service marks.

Before filing, confirm:

- actual owner/applicant;
- goods/services descriptions;
- first-use dates and specimens where required;
- conflicts with existing marks;
- jurisdictions worth protecting.

Repository use of ™ can identify a claimed mark but is not equivalent to registration.

## 5. Clarify chain of title

Create and retain signed private records establishing who owns and who may license each material right, including as applicable:

- source-code copyrights;
- documentation and artwork copyrights;
- trademarks and service marks;
- domain names and key brand assets;
- inventions and patent rights;
- contractor/employee-created materials;
- contributor rights needed for future dual licensing.

Public repository wording should not be treated as a substitute for executed assignments or licenses.

## 6. Register high-value copyrights where useful

With counsel, evaluate copyright registration for high-value original works such as source-code releases, documentation, graphics, and other protectable expression where registration would materially improve enforcement options.

Copyright does not protect an abstract idea, system, method, short phrase, or functional concept merely because it is described in this repository.

## 7. Patent review before additional disclosure

If a genuinely novel technical invention may be commercially important, obtain patent advice before additional public disclosure.

Public GitHub releases, papers, demos, blogs, presentations, sales materials, and public experiments can affect patent rights and filing deadlines. Rules differ by jurisdiction; do not assume a U.S. grace period preserves rights elsewhere.

This repository must not claim that a patent exists unless an actual filing or issued patent supports that claim.

## 8. Preserve trade-secret boundaries

Do not place material in the public repository if commercial value depends on secrecy.

Potential confidential layers may include:

- production private signing keys;
- non-public enterprise policy packs;
- proprietary managed-service logic;
- confidential customer configurations;
- customer datasets;
- private pricing and negotiated commercial terms;
- security-sensitive deployment information;
- unreleased inventions being evaluated for patent protection.

Trade-secret protection depends in part on reasonable secrecy measures; publishing material publicly defeats secrecy for the published material.

## 9. Control future contributor rights

Current contribution terms should not be interpreted as silently transferring contributor ownership.

Before relying on outside contributions in code that ĀRU may later want to offer under a different commercial license, determine whether a contributor license agreement or other written permission is appropriate.

Do not claim a right to relicense third-party contributions beyond rights actually granted.

## 10. Build the paid layer around rights ĀRU actually controls

Potential revenue layers can include:

- official compatibility-brand authorization;
- signed official authorization credentials;
- enterprise architecture and implementation;
- OEM/embedded programs;
- managed verification, policy, receipt, trust, transparency, and revocation infrastructure;
- private deployment tooling;
- enterprise support and training;
- separately licensed future products or components;
- strategic integrations and partnerships.

The public MIT implementation can accelerate adoption while these controlled layers remain separately commercialized.

## 11. Use written agreements for commercial rights

Do not rely on GitHub issues, email discussions, test results, or public registry entries as substitutes for contracts where a commercial relationship requires one.

Commercial agreements should define the actual scope, including as relevant:

- licensed marks;
- authorized products/services;
- territory;
- term and termination;
- quality-control requirements;
- fees;
- support/services;
- confidentiality;
- data/security obligations;
- warranty/liability terms;
- revocation or credential status;
- ownership of new work and improvements.

## 12. Review before major licensing changes

Before changing the repository’s core license, introducing source-available restrictions, dual licensing, or moving material between open and proprietary layers, review:

- rights already granted under prior releases;
- contributor ownership;
- third-party dependencies;
- package-manager metadata;
- release artifacts;
- documentation promises;
- downstream expectations;
- commercial objectives.

A licensing change should be prospective, explicit, technically enforceable where relevant, and legally supportable.
