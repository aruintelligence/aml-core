# The ĀML Manifesto

## The browser learned to display documents. It never learned to justify attention.

HTML was a breakthrough. It gave the world a shared document structure and helped turn a network into a civilization-scale publishing system.

But a document language from the early web should not be the final accountability architecture for intelligent interfaces.

Modern products do more than publish. They interrupt, persuade, rank, recommend, personalize, stimulate, and retain. Yet the dominant rendering contract remains remarkably permissive: if the markup exists and the syntax is valid, the element can appear.

That is not enough.

## ĀML proposes a second contract

ĀML™—ĀRU Meaning Language™—asks developers to declare more than layout.

An interface element should be able to expose:

- what it means;
- what purpose it serves;
- what attention it expects;
- what restorative value its author claims;
- what policy evaluated it;
- whether it was allowed, degraded, or suppressed;
- and why that decision occurred.

This does not make a machine morally authoritative. It makes the machine's policy inputs visible enough to inspect, test, contest, and improve.

## From permissionless rendering to accountable rendering

The current prototype begins with a deliberately legible gate:

```text
render_allowed = restoration_value ≥ attention_cost
```

The equation is not presented as human truth. It is a testable policy model. Its value is that it turns an invisible design assumption into an explicit computational object.

An invisible assumption cannot be audited. A declared assumption can.

## ĀML is not anti-HTML

ĀML compiles to the web platform today. HTML remains the delivery substrate.

The challenge is not that HTML is useless. The challenge is that structure alone cannot carry the accountability modern interfaces require. ĀML adds a meaning tree, a policy decision, and a machine-readable audit trail before conventional rendering.

HTML says: **this is a component.**

ĀML asks: **what is it for, what does it cost, and did it earn the right to appear?**

## Our commitments

We will treat claims as claims, measurements as measurements, and metaphors as metaphors.

We will expose model inputs and decision outputs.

We will invite counterexamples, alternative policies, accessibility critique, and adversarial testing.

We will not describe prototype scores as validated measures of cognition, wellbeing, ethics, or harm.

We will pursue interfaces that preserve orientation, reduce fragmentation, and respect finite human attention.

## The invitation

ĀML is early. That is precisely why its foundations should be debated in public.

Run the compiler. Break the parser. Challenge the equation. Replace the policy. Test the output. Improve the language.

The future of interfaces should not be decided only by what increases engagement.

It should also be shaped by what deserves attention.
